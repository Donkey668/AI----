Migratory animals, especially birds, have evolved a suite of behaviors associated with journeys between breeding and non-breeding sites [1]. Although migration itself can be costly and dangerous, it allows animals to seasonally access abundant food supplies [2], reduce predation risk [3], avoid unfavorable climatic conditions and decrease competition, thus maximizing individual fitness [4]. However, these benefits of migration depend on relatively predictable environmental conditions among years [5]. Migratory animals can be vulnerable to rapid and significant environmental changes, with any stage in their life cycle potentially at risk [6].

Migratory birds that exhibit high variability within a population in terms of migratory routes and destinations tend to be more resilient to population declines [7–9]. This suggests that a diversity of migration strategies among individuals can mitigate the impact of environmental change on populations [7]. At the individual level, the exploration-refinement hypothesis predicts that young birds will follow more exploratory routes at first, but enhance their migratory efficiency through learning and experience accumulation, then gradually shorten and refine their migration route as they age, settling on a preferred route as adults [10–13]. However, different individuals may settle on different preferred routes based on their unique exploration experiences, and the continuous recruitment of young, exploratory individuals into the population maintains this diversity. Therefore, while migration diversity is maintained at a population level, this process generally leads to increased route consistency and reduced variability in annual movement patterns at the individual level. Consequently, strong site fidelity and route specialization may pose risks for some individuals if their preferred habitats become unavailable, particularly when alternative suitable habitats exist but remain unexplored.

However, some species, such as Greater Flamingo (Phoenicopterus roseus; [14]), Black Kite (Milvus migrans; [15]), and Marsh Harrier (Circus aeruginosus; [16]), are known to retain a certain degree of behavioral flexibility into adulthood. In these species, adult individuals may modify their migration routes or timing in response to changes in temperature, wind conditions, and habitat availability, rather than becoming increasingly fixed in their patterns with age. This flexibility is particularly important given the extensive conversion of natural habitats into artificial environments [17]. While some species can adjust their habitat selection and foraging strategies to utilize newly created artificial habitats, this ability varies greatly among species. For instance, waterbird populations that extensively utilize aquaculture farms are less likely to experience population declines [17]. In contrast, species lacking such adaptive flexibility, particularly threatened species such as Spoon-billed Sandpiper (Calidris pygmaea), struggle to cope with habitat transformations [18, 19]. Understanding the factors that determine migratory behavioral flexibility is therefore crucial for predicting species’ responses to ongoing environmental changes. The ontogenetic development of migratory behavior is essential to assess species’ adaptability, as it reveals whether behavioral flexibility is maintained throughout an individual’s lifetime or becomes constrained with age. This has important implications for how populations might respond to environmental changes.

More than half of the world’s waterbird species with known trends are declining [20], but the Pied Avocet (Recurvirostra avosetta), a typical wetland waterbird that can utilize artificial habitats [21, 22], has a wide distribution and is showing rapid population growth in the East Asian-Australasian Flyway and other parts of the world [23–25]. The Pied Avocet can establish new breeding sites at considerable distances from previous sites across years, with maximum recorded distances exceeding 1000 km [26, 27], suggesting an unusually high degree of individual migration behavioral flexibility in this species. Recent banded studies on European populations have established that Pied Avocets exhibit partial migration, with individuals showing high fidelity to their first wintering strategy while experiencing moderate age-related changes in migratory behavior [28, 29]. While these banding studies provided valuable insights into wintering site fidelity, they offer limited information on detailed migratory routes, timing, and behavioral flexibility throughout the annual cycle.

Migration timing and distance are critical variables in quantifying migration patterns [30]. The latitudes of breeding and wintering grounds usually represent the northernmost and southernmost regions occupied by the species. For instance, more competitive adults often select higher latitude wintering grounds, enabling them to return to breeding grounds earlier to secure better nesting sites [31]. Furthermore, home range size is a crucial component of movement ecology [32, 33], as it can indicate individual exploratory behavior. Particularly under changing environmental conditions, individuals with higher exploratory tendencies typically possess larger home ranges and greater adaptability [34].

In this study, we first examine how breeding success affects nest site selection in subsequent years by comparing inter-annual nest distances between successful and unsuccessful breeding adults, to determine whether breeding failure leads to greater site switching behavior. Subsequently, we compare five aspects of the movement behaviors of juvenile and adult Pied Avocets to investigate how migration decisions vary with age: (i) migration departure and arrival dates, (ii) migration distance, (iii) latitude of breeding and wintering grounds, (iv) ranging behaviors during the pre-migration period, and (v) within-season movements at the wintering grounds and breeding grounds. We hypothesize that while migration timing will synchronize with age due to its critical importance for survival and reproduction [4], spatial aspects of migration behavior will retain individual variability as individuals age and gain experience, rather than becoming increasingly stereotyped.

During the breeding seasons of 2019 to 2021, we captured 48 adult Pied Avocets (≥ 2 years) and 20 juveniles hatched in 2021 in the Bohai region of eastern China (Fig. 1).

Forty-five adult Pied Avocets were captured using walk-in traps or clap nets during the incubation period. Other adults and fledged juveniles were captured using clap nets at their roosting sites before the middle of August when most individuals had not dispersed from the breeding areas. In their first year, juveniles were differentiated from adults based on plumage [35]. In 2021, three of the 20 juvenile Pied Avocets tracked in Hangu were artificially incubated from abandoned eggs, then were raised in a saltpan cage until fledging. Post-release observations showed that their behavior was indistinguishable from that of wild juveniles. We outfitted them with tracking devices using a leg-loop harness [36]. Two types of transmitters were used: (1) HQPG2009P (N = 43, 9 g solar GPS/GSM, Hunan Global Messenger Technology Ltd., Changsha City, China) and (2) HQPG 1206 (N = 25, 7 g solar GPS/GSM). Sampling frequencies were automatically adjusted based on battery levels to prevent battery depletion and ensure continuous tracking. Both models switched from their maximum frequency (2 h for HQPG2009P; 3 h for HQPG 1206) to 6 h and then 12 h intervals as voltage dropped. All transmitters weighed less than 3% of the bird’s body weight (see Wu et al. [27] for specifications). We captured birds across four breeding sites separated by less than 300 km. GPS tracking revealed that birds moved between these sites during the breeding season. The juveniles were classified as first-year (age 1) from birth until their arrival at the breeding area of the next year, and then second-year (age 2) and so on.

Therefore, the first year of life contains the first southward migration and the first northward migration. Given that it is hard to age Pied Avocets by plumage after their second summer in the wild, we excluded adults banded in 2021 (they could be age 2) when preparing the data to ensure that only third-year and older adults (age ≥ 3) were included in our analysis. All age 2 individuals included in our analysis were already being tracked when they were age 1. Tracking data were downloaded from the HQXS service platform (https://download.hqxs-tracker.com/), selecting accuracy grades A, B, and C, which correspond to horizontal positioning errors of ≤ 5 m, ≤ 10 m, and ≤ 20 m, respectively [37]. In contrast, grade D (≤ 100 m) was excluded to ensure sufficient spatial precision for subsequent analyses. We analyzed data collected from 2021 to 2024.

While fledging is theoretically the better indicator of breeding success, Pied Avocets produce precocial young that immediately leave the nest with their parents after hatching [38], sometimes with only one parent (Wu, personal observation), making systematic post-hatch monitoring impractical without direct observation. Moreover, hatching fate itself drives nest-site fidelity decisions in birds following the “win-stay lose-switch’ strategy”: Snowy Plovers (Charadrius nivosus) and Emperor Geese (Anser canagicus) that hatched eggs showed high site fidelity, while failed breeders dispersed farther [39, 40]. Therefore, we used hatching success as our measure of breeding success. We analyzed satellite data using the “find_nests” function in “nestR” package [41] to extract potential nest sites. The “find_nests” function identifies nest locations by detecting clusters of GPS fixes where individuals showed sustained site fidelity. We set the breeding season from April 1 to September 1 (sea_start = “04 − 01”, sea_end = “9 − 01”). A spatial buffer of 20 m was used to define nest site boundaries to account for the maximum positioning error of the tracking data (Grade C, ≤ 20 m). Following automated detection, we manually inspected candidate sites to exclude false positives (e.g., frequently visited shallow water foraging areas). We had 39 individuals whose nest fates were confirmed through infrared camera (Yianws L710, Yianws, Shenzhen, China) monitoring and who had complete tracking data during their incubation period as well. Cameras were cable-tied to wooden stakes positioned approximately 1 m from the nests and programmed to capture images at 1-minute intervals throughout the incubation period to document hatching success or nest failure events. Based on data from these 39 nests and the incubation period of 24 days from laying the last egg to hatching [42], we established a threshold of “consec_days” (the duration of the longest stretch of consecutive days when the nest location was visited) > 24 days to determine successful hatching. Subsequently, we used the “geosphere” package [43] to calculate distances between nest sites in consecutive years.

Migration timing refers to the specific periods or dates when birds start and complete their migratory journeys, including the departure and arrival times at breeding and wintering sites. Following Wu et al. [27], stopover sites were defined as locations where birds remained within 50 km for more than 48 h during migration. Departure and arrival times were determined as the last and first locations at breeding/wintering areas with > 48 h residence time, respectively. When the migration tracking signal was lost over 24 h but less than 10 d, the starting date of migration was defined as the median date between the date of the last location in the breeding area and the first location during migration. and individuals whose signal was lost over 10 d during migration were excluded [27]. We calculated the stopover sites, departure and arrival time for the migratory movements of birds between 2021 and 2023 using Julian dates, where the first day of each year is designated as day 1. We used the R package “geosphere” to calculate the distance between the last location of all breeding/stopover sites before migration and the first location when arriving at the next stopping/wintering sites. Migration distance was calculated as the cumulative great-circle distance (the shortest distance between two points on a sphere) traveled from breeding site through all stopover sites to wintering site.

We analyzed the average latitude of each individual at wintering and breeding grounds as key spatial metric, calculating mean latitudes for different age classes. For brevity, we refer, e.g. to the winter of 2021/2022 as 2021 in this study. To ensure analytical robustness while maximizing sample size, we included only individuals with data records on more than 30 days within these periods. This threshold was selected to exclude transient or incomplete observations while retaining sufficient samples for age comparisons, and represents approximately one-third of the typical residency period at these sites for this species. The resulting average recording duration was 87.2 ± 4.6 days (age 1), 83.5 ± 8.4 days (age 2), and 79.7 ± 12.4 days (age ≥ 3) for wintering grounds, and 102.5 ± 29.8 days (age 2) and 107.8 ± 24.7 days (age ≥ 3) for breeding grounds. At breeding grounds, we compared only age 2 and age ≥ 3 individuals, as most age 1 birds were banded in August when their locations were still influenced by parental care and did not represent independent breeding site selection.

Incubation is typically completed by the end of July, and adult Pied Avocets leave the breeding grounds in October [27]. Juveniles fledge 32 to 42 days post-hatching and subsequently forage independently from the adults [44]. Consequently, by the end of August, adults who are living independently can roam freely over a month before the start of migration, and adults whose breeding attempt failed can roam for even more time. Meanwhile, juveniles may increase flight activities before migration to gain flight experience and enhance their subsequent survival rates [45]. Thus, we compared the pre-migration activities of Pied Avocets of different ages by calculating the 95% Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) home range one month before their southward migration from 2021 to 2023. Within this period, we only included data for birds in a stationary state (speed < 5 km per hour). Given the variability in sampling frequency (2–12 h) and the number of location points among individuals (1-568 points), we conducted random sampling to facilitate comparison. We plotted the accumulation curve of KDE area relative to the number of sampled locations, determining the minimum threshold where the area estimate becomes relatively stable (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1). KDE analysis was performed using the kernelUD function in the “adehabitatHR” package in R [46], with smoothing factors (h) calculated using the reference bandwidth method (href). For individuals with location points exceeding this threshold, we randomly extracted the threshold number of points, calculated the home range area size, and repeated the process 100 times to obtain an average area value. This standardization ensured that home range estimates were comparable across individuals by controlling for differences in sampling effort [47]. The resulting estimates were then log-transformed to achieve a normal distribution for the next analysis.

The KDE areas focus on the main habitat, potentially excluding “outlier” data. However, these “outliers” may reflect exploratory behavior or other within-season nomadic movements. For example, during the breeding season, individuals may arrive at Bohai Bay, travel northward, and then return to Bohai Bay before beginning their southward migration, and during wintering, some individuals make movements between inland and coastal sites. To account for these movements, we calculated the distance between the first location and the farthest location reached during the wintering and breeding seasons, respectively. Prior to analysis, we visually inspected individual movement tracks to remove erroneous locations. However, no such locations were identified, and all original data were retained for analysis. Only individuals with complete data coverage during specific periods were included.

We used linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) and generalized linear models (GLMs) to analyze migration patterns and space use across age groups (Table 1), with age as the fixed effect and individual ID as a random effect in all mixed models to account for repeated measurements from the same individuals. Model assumptions were assessed visually using Q-Q plots for normality and scatterplots of residuals versus fitted values for homoscedasticity. For migration timing (departure and arrival dates) and migration distance, we fitted LMMs with Julian date or distance as the response variable as these continuous variables approximated normal distributions. For home range size, we used LMMs with log-transformed 95% KDE as the response variable to normalize the right-skewed distribution typical of area data. For average latitude at wintering grounds, we used LMMs with latitude as the response variable. For average latitude at breeding grounds, we used the Mann-Whitney U test because the data violated the assumption of normality (Shapiro-Wilk test, p < 0.05). For within-season shifts at breeding and wintering grounds, we used GLMs with gamma distribution and log link to accommodate the strictly positive and right-skewed nature of the shift distance data. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05.

Our study reveals a paradoxical pattern in Pied Avocet migration behavior: contrary to the exploration-refinement hypothesis, adults exhibited greater movement variability and ranging behavior than juveniles. The exploration-refinement hypothesis posits that inexperienced individuals initially explore widely, then progressively refine their migratory routes, timing, and destinations as they age, resulting in reduced spatial variability and increased route fidelity in adults [10, 12]. This pattern has been documented across multiple taxa: Juvenile Cyprus Wheatears (Oenanthe cypriaca) migrated via less direct routes than adults in autumn, and non-breeding locations were more repeatable for adults than for juveniles [49]; White Storks (Ciconia ciconia) switched from exploration to rapid and directed movement as they aged, with older individuals ceasing to explore new places and instead moving more quickly and directly [10]; Cory’s Shearwaters (Calonectris borealis) gradually shortened and stabilized their routes during maturation [11]; and juvenile Black-tailed Godwits (Limosa limosa) exhibited more variable routes than adults [9]. While our finding on migration timing aligned with this framework, with timing synchronizing with age as reported in previous studies [49–52], spatial flexibility unexpectedly increased rather than decreased with age. Adults occupied significantly lower wintering latitudes, had larger pre-migration home ranges, and showed greater within-season movements than younger birds. This reverse ontogenetic pattern challenges our understanding of how migratory behavior develops in long-lived migratory birds.

While juvenile migratory birds typically migrate farther than adults to more remote wintering locations [4, 53, 54], this age-reversed pattern in our study may reflect distinct survival-reproduction trade-offs across life stages in this species. Juveniles’ conservative strategy, utilizing traditional wintering grounds and maintaining smaller ranges, likely minimizes mortality risks during the critical early life period. The “closer-to-home” strategy has been associated with higher juvenile survival in other migratory species [55]. Once individuals reach adulthood and have accumulated sufficient experience, the benefits of exploratory behavior may outweigh its risks. This pattern suggests an alternative life-history strategy where behavioral flexibility increases rather than decreases with age, potentially reflecting adaptation to unpredictable environments.

The high individual variability among adults suggests a population-level bet-hedging strategy rather than uniform behavioral optimization. While most adults maintained relatively consistent routes across years, as previously documented in this species [27], a subset showed remarkable flexibility that deviated substantially from typical patterns. These individuals exhibited movements to latitudes as far north as 50°N (compared to the adult population mean of 39.5 ± 1.7°N) and extensive within-season shifts exceeding 1,400 km (compared to the adult mean of 323.0 ± 315.2 km). This flexibility appears to be at least intrinsic rather than reactive, as hatching failure did not predict subsequent nest relocation distance. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that individuals responded to other environmental or social cues not measured in this study, such as local habitat changes, prey availability, or conspecific density and distribution that may serve as inadvertent social information for site selection [56, 57]. Future studies incorporating fine-scale environmental monitoring and social network analyses would help disentangle intrinsic behavioral tendencies from reactive responses to environmental conditions. Nevertheless, the maintenance of both conservative and exploratory phenotypes within the adult population could enhance population resilience, with exploratory individuals potentially establishing new breeding sites while conservative individuals maintain population stability at traditional sites.

Adults’ larger home ranges during the month preceding southward migration may reflect the adaptive significance of exploratory behavior. As a long-lived species with lifespans exceeding 20 years, adult Pied Avocets’ exploratory behavior may help assess potential breeding sites [58–60]. Individual S44 provides longitudinal evidence for this age-dependent behavioral transition: this bird maintained conservative behavior in its first three years, then dramatically expanded its range in the fourth year, reaching remote inland regions (Fig. S2). This trajectory from conservative to exploratory strategies supports our cross-sectional comparison results.

The extensive exploratory behavior of a few adult individuals should not be dismissed as meaningless. Even seemingly “misoriented” individuals may be crucial for establishing new populations [61]. Wijethunge et al. (2024) independently documented similar flexible migration behavior in adult Pied Avocets [62], suggesting this is a widespread characteristic. Such behavioral flexibility can confer selective advantages, as seen in Yellow-legged Gulls (Larus michahellis), which develop individual specialization through behavioral flexibility, thereby reducing intraspecific competition and exploiting novel resources from human activities [63].

For Pied Avocets, this flexibility’s ecological significance may be closely linked to habitat changes. Over the past 50 years, extensive artificial wetlands including salt ponds and aquaculture facilities have emerged in key areas like Bohai Bay [64]. These artificial wetlands provide alternative breeding habitats and foraging sites for Pied Avocets [22]. The species’ ability to utilize these new habitats may be a key factor in its population growth in East Asia [25, 65], while most other shorebird populations are declining [66]. Therefore, the exploratory behavior maintained by adults, even if expressed in only a few individuals, may serve a “pioneer” role, opening new living spaces for the population.

Several limitations constrain our interpretations. First, age groups were not the same individuals tracked continuously; the age ≥ 3 group combined different cohorts, which not only prevented ontogenetic analysis but may also have obscured age-related patterns by pooling individuals at different life stages. Second, small sample sizes, particularly for age 2 birds, limited statistical power. Third, methodological constraints including tagging biases, unequal tracking durations among individuals, and temporal gaps in GPS data may have introduced variation unrelated to the biological patterns of interest. These limitations highlight the need for long-term individual tracking from juvenile through adult stages with standardized deployment protocols. Nevertheless, the consistent age-related patterns in migration timing and the statistically significant differences in spatial flexibility provide robust support for our main conclusions; indeed, these limitations are more likely to mask real differences than to produce false results, suggesting our findings may underestimate the true age-related differences.

These findings highlight the importance of age-structured behavioral diversity for population resilience. However, establishing whether behavioral flexibility directly contributes to the Pied Avocet’s success requires integrating movement data with habitat quality assessments, survival analysis, and population modeling. Future research should examine whether similar age-reversed patterns occur in other thriving species, test whether “pioneer” individuals indeed establish viable populations in novel habitats and assess how maintaining landscape heterogeneity affects population persistence. Understanding the ontogenetic development of behavioral flexibility is crucial for predicting which species will successfully adapt to ongoing environmental change.
